## The size of it, and the drawdown as lived

| Claim | When | Conf. | Source | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The active Army stood at about 610,000 at the start of fiscal 1993 and 572,423 at its end on September 30, 1993 - the lowest since 1948; with it the Army Reserve at 275,900, the National Guard at 409,919 and 294,217 civilians. (The Defense Manpower Data Center's own count of the same day reads 568,529; institutions.md carries it.) A year earlier the fall had been from 710,233 to 610,450, about 14 percent in one year. | FY1991 to FY1993 | HIGH | DAHSUM93, ch. 3, Table 4; DAHSUM92, ch. 7, Table 9 | service |
| The whole force, end of fiscal year, in thousands: 1990 2,044 (Army 732); 1991 1,986 (711); 1992 1,807 (610); 1993 1,705 (572); 1994 1,610 (541). The plan handed to the base-closure commission had the Army at 538,000 by 1995 and 522,000 by 1997. | FY1990 to FY1994 | HIGH | Census Bureau, Statistical Abstract of the United States 1997, Table 557 (Department of Defense, Selected Manpower Statistics); Defense Base Closure and Realignment Commission, 1993 Report to the President, Appendix B-5 | everyone (that it was shrinking); service (the numbers) |
| At the end of fiscal 1993 the Army had four corps, fourteen active divisions and eight Guard divisions; the post-Cold War cut so far was a quarter of the active force, one corps and four divisions gone. The Bottom-Up Review of September 1 told it to go on down to ten active divisions, and the reserve structure to five divisions and 37 combat brigades. | 1993-09 | HIGH | DAHSUM93, ch. 1 and ch. 4; Roanoke Times (Washington Post and AP), "Military to Shrink," Sept 2, 1993 | service |
| Getting out was paid for: the Voluntary Separation Incentive and the Special Separation Benefit took effect in December 1991 for people with six to twenty years' service. In fiscal 1992, 29,134 soldiers left the Army under them (51,578 across the Department; 87 percent took the lump sum), and the Army used the incentives most of any service. About 100,000 soldiers left the Army that year, more than 52,000 of them under the incentive and early-transition programs. | 1991-12 to FY1992 | HIGH | General Accounting Office, "Military Downsizing: Balancing Accessions and Losses Is Key to Shaping the Future Force," GAO/NSIAD-93-241, Sept 1993, pp. 25-26; DAHSUM92, ch. 7 | service |
| In fiscal 1993, 107,627 enlisted soldiers and 14,967 officers left; 3,469 under the separation incentives; about 1,300 sergeants in overstrength specialties were offered early retirement and 241 took it; 2,371 were separated for failing body-fat standards. Recruiting still brought in 77,563, just over its goal. | FY1993 | HIGH | DAHSUM93, ch. 3 | service |

## Fort Knox in 1993

| Claim | When | Conf. | Source | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fort Knox was the Home of Armor: the Armored Force was headquartered there in the summer of 1940, and the Armor Center and School stayed until the 2005 base-closure round sent them to Fort Benning. | 1940 to 2011 | HIGH | U.S. Army Fort Knox, "History" (home.army.mil/knox) | service:armor; region:Kentucky |
| Major General Paul E. Funk was the Chief of Armor and the school's commandant from 1992 - the 34th, returning to Fort Knox after commanding the 3rd Armored Division in the Gulf; he had commanded the 194th Armored Brigade there earlier. The 1993 issues of the branch magazine still list him as commandant. | 1992-07 (1993 by index) | HIGH for 1992; MEDIUM (index) for 1993 | ARMOR magazine, July-August 1992, masthead and pp. 2-3 (a DVIDS-hosted copy); the 1993 issues on the Armor School's own archive (host unreachable; index) | service:armor |
| The post's units: the 194th Separate Armored Brigade (which sent elements to Desert Storm; reorganized at Knox to support the school and inactivated there on June 29, 1995); the 16th Cavalry Regiment, reactivated in April 1993 as the training unit for all leader training at the Armor School; the 1st Armor Training Brigade for one-station unit training, to which a Training Group was attached as 3rd Battalion, 81st Armor on July 30, 1993. Fort Knox and Fort Campbell together sent 21,500 people to the Gulf. | 1993 | HIGH for the Gulf and the 194th's deployment; MEDIUM (index) for the 1993 reorganizations | Kentucky Department of Military Affairs, "The Kentucky National Guard in the Persian Gulf War 1990-1991," introduction; Fort Moore unit histories for the 194th Brigade, 3-16 Cavalry and 3-81 Armor (host unreachable; index) | service:armor |
| A base-closure realignment in progress in fiscal 1993 was moving the Army Recruiting Command from Fort Sheridan, Illinois, to Fort Knox. | FY1993 | HIGH | DAHSUM93, ch. 2 | service |
| The Patton Museum, open since May 30, 1949, is on the post; the gold is not the Army's - the Treasury's Kentucky Bullion Depository stands adjacent to Fort Knox, closed to visitors, the Mint's deep storage since 1937 (the mechanized 1st Cavalry guarded the first shipments in). | 1937; 1949 | HIGH | U.S. Army Fort Knox, "History" and "Visitor Information"; Bureau of the Fiscal Service, "Status Report of U.S. Treasury-Owned Gold" | everyone |
| The post covers 108,955 acres in Hardin, Meade and Bullitt counties (a current figure; the 1993 acreage and population were not read). | current | MEDIUM | U.S. Army Fort Knox, "Visitor Information" | region:Kentucky |

## The 1993 base-closure round

| Claim | When | Conf. | Source | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Defense Base Closure and Realignment Commission (chairman Jim Courter, seven commissioners, seventeen hearings, over 125 site visits) reported to the President on July 1, 1993 from a Secretary's list of 31 major bases; on July 2 the President forwarded it to Congress with a five-point conversion plan (a million-dollar grant on average for each major-closure community, one federal coordinator, faster cleanup and property disposal) and said some 90 overseas bases would close too. The papers counted 35 major closures. | 1993-07-01 to 07-02 | HIGH | Defense Base Closure and Realignment Commission, "1993 Report to the President," July 1, 1993 (govinfo); Weekly Compilation of Presidential Documents, vol. 29, no. 26, "Remarks Announcing the Defense Conversion Plan and an Exchange With Reporters," July 2, 1993, pp. 1219-1220; Roanoke Times (Los Angeles Times), "Clinton OKs Base Closings," July 2, 1993, and "Clinton Offsets Closings," July 3, 1993 | everyone; service |
| Fort Knox appears nowhere in the 1993 report. The Army outcomes were elsewhere: Fort McClellan kept its Chemical and Military Police Schools against the Department's proposal; Fort Monmouth was kept with the Communications-Electronics Command moved onto it and the Chaplain School sent to Fort Jackson; Vint Hill Farms closed; Letterkenny Depot stayed open; Tooele Depot was cut to a depot activity; Sixth Army stayed at the Presidio of San Francisco. Earlier rounds were already closing Fort Benjamin Harrison, Fort Ord, Fort Devens and Sacramento Army Depot, and moving the 5th Infantry Division from Fort Polk to Fort Hood. | 1993-07-01 | HIGH | Defense Base Closure and Realignment Commission, "1993 Report to the President," ch. 1 and the closure list (full-text search); DAHSUM93, ch. 1-2 | service; region:Kentucky (that Knox was spared) |

## What a soldier of 1993 had been through

| Claim | When | Conf. | Source | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Somalia: the operation began December 9, 1992 with the Marine landing; the 2nd Battalion, 87th Infantry of the 10th Mountain Division deployed the next day; U.S. forces peaked above 25,800 on January 16, 1993; the last regional handover, at Merca on April 28, went from the 10th Mountain's 1-22 Infantry to a Pakistani battalion with 6,650 Americans left, mostly logisticians; Restore Hope ended May 4 when the UN took over, with about 5,000 U.S. troops staying. The Army counted 73 casualties in Somalia in fiscal 1993, eight of them killed by hostile action. | 1992-12-09 to 1993-09 | HIGH | DAHSUM93, ch. 1, 5, 6 and 8; Roanoke Times (Los Angeles Times), "U.S. Hands Over Somalia Command," Apr 29, 1993 | service; everyone as a headline |
| Los Angeles, 1992: after the April 29 verdict the President federalized part of the California Guard on May 1 and sent active forces; Joint Task Force Los Angeles was about 10,000 guardsmen, nearly 1,500 Marines from Camp Pendleton and 2,000 soldiers of the 7th Infantry Division's 2nd Brigade from Fort Ord; the curfew lifted May 4 and the troops were gone by May 6. Fifty-eight dead, over 2,000 injured, a billion dollars in losses. | 1992-04-29 to 05-06 | HIGH | DAHSUM92, ch. 3 | service; everyone |
| The flood of 1993: flood stage was passed at about 500 gauging points across nine states; the upper Mississippi closed to navigation for over two months; the Corps of Engineers worked its 76 reservoirs in the basin and reckoned its works prevented $19.1 billion of further damage; by July 11 Illinois alone had 2,700 guardsmen out, sixteen people were dead since late June, and most Missouri-side levees between Hannibal and St. Louis had gone. | 1993-06 to 09 | HIGH | DAHSUM93, ch. 9; Roanoke Times (AP and Washington Post), "Midwest's Ocean Widens," July 11, 1993 | service; region:Midwest as lived |
| On Wednesday, April 28, 1993, Secretary Aspin lifted the ban on women in combat aviation, effective at once, asked Congress to repeal the law barring women from Navy combat ships, and ordered the services to reconsider the other exclusions - all but front-line infantry and armor. | 1993-04-28 | HIGH | Roanoke Times (Washington Post), "Female War Role to Grow," Apr 29, 1993 | service; service:armor (the exception) |

## The Kentucky Guard

| Claim | When | Conf. | Source | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| In the Gulf War eight Kentucky Guard units, about 1,147 people, deployed (1,078 Army Guard soldiers in the desert): the 1st Battalion, 623rd Field Artillery out of Glasgow, Tompkinsville, Campbellsville, Monticello and Springfield (433 soldiers, one of the few Guard combat-arms units sent); the 2123rd Transportation Company (Bowling Green and Richmond); the 475th MASH and the 133rd Public Affairs Detachment (Frankfort); the 137th Transportation and 217th Quartermaster detachments; the 223rd and 438th Military Police Companies out of Louisville; the 123rd Tactical Airlift Wing's C-130s in support; six Kentuckians died.
